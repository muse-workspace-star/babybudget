How Much Does a Baby Cost in 2026?

The short answer, the numbers behind it, and what drives your total up or down. All figures are US survey averages — estimates for planning, not quotes.

The national average

BabyCenter's 2025 research anchors the picture:

  • $20,384 — average first-year baby cost.
  • $15,000 — what budget-conscious families typically spend.
  • $50,000+ — premium gear plus full-time nanny care.

The range is enormous because two families' choices barely overlap. The three levers that move your number most are childcare type, feeding approach, and gear tier.

Childcare: the biggest line item

For working parents, childcare is typically 40–60% of the first-year total. National monthly averages for infant care (2026):

State spreads are dramatic: infant center care runs ~$650/month in Mississippi and ~$2,400/month in Washington DC. Get local quotes during pregnancy — quality infant spots carry 6–18 month waitlists.

Feeding: $360 to $3,000 a year

What feeding costs over 12 months, by approach:

Breastfeeding isn't free — budget for pump parts, storage bags, and nursing supplies. And never dilute formula to save money; the health risk isn't worth it.

Diapers, gear & health

  • Diapers: $60–$120/month for disposables ($450–$1,200+ over the first year; babies use 2,500–3,000). Cloth costs $300–$800 upfront and can save $500+.
  • Gear (one-time): ~$1,200 budget (secondhand basics), ~$2,800 mid-range (new crib, stroller, car seat), ~$6,000 premium. The Bump estimates ~$2,000 for a nursery set alone.
  • Health insurance: KFF's 2025 survey puts the average employer family premium at $26,993, with workers contributing $6,850 — adding a child raises your share.

What pushes costs up (and down)

  • Up: full-time nanny, premium formula, top-brand gear bought new, high-cost metro childcare market.
  • Down: family childcare, breastfeeding, cloth diapers, secondhand gear, store-brand consumables bought in bulk.
Hidden costs to budget for: daycare registration and supply fees ($100–$400+), full tuition during holiday closures, the health-premium bump when you add your child, and a 4% contingency fund — see the budget breakdown guide.
